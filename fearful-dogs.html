Fearful dogs

Recognition and behaviour modification

RSPCA Victoria Behaviour Program • For shelter staff and volunteers

How to use this guide

Work through the sections in order. Each one builds on the previous. Do not skip to slip lead or touching until distance testing and consent testing are complete. Tap any section to expand it.

Rule of thumbIf a dog cannot take treats, it is over threshold. Stop the session, give the dog space, and try again at a quieter time.

Canine Fear Scale

Score the dog before each session and record the level in your session note below. Use this scale to guide your handling approach and to track progress over time.

🟢 Neutral — H
BodyRelaxed body; soft gaze; low or neutral tail wag; relaxed ears and mouth.
BehaviourCalm; consents to gentle petting; willingly approaches; no escape attempts.
HandlingNo defensive handling needed.
🟡 Mild fear — H/TR
BodySlightly tense body; tail low and may or may not wag; ears may be back; submissive gestures including lip lick, lifted paw, low fast tail wag, or submissive urination.
BehaviourMild hesitation; cautious movement; startles but recovers within seconds; may stress yawn, lip lick, or fidget. May eat despite the presence of the trigger.
HandlingCalm, slow approach. Avoid over-petting. May avoid but recovers quickly.
🟠 Moderate fear — TR/TM
BodyTense and rigid; tail low or tucked; ears back; whale eye or gaze diversion; may crouch, tremble, or lean backward; may alarm bark.
BehaviourDoes not actively explore; may remain immobile or pace looking for an escape route. Prolonged recovery from startles. May or may not eat with the trigger present.
HandlingAllow 48 to 72 hours to bond with a small number of familiar attendants before proceeding with health checks. Avoid pushing contact. Chill protocol may be required. AA to follow Fear of Touch DS/CC and Consent Testing protocols in this guide.
🔴 Severe fear — TM/UU
BodyVery tense or rigid; tail low and tucked; ears flat; whale eye; body against the ground or crouched; trembling; closed-mouth or rapid breathing; may growl.
BehaviourFreeze, hide, or shut down (catatonic); extreme escape attempts; may scream, defecate, or urinate. Defensive or offensive aggression possible (see Recognition section).
HandlingHandling must be avoided unless essential. Use protected contact only. Flag immediately to kennel team leader and Vet AA for BMOD assessment and consideration of psychotropic medication. Do not attempt to interact with dogs showing offensive aggression.
Tips for useFear is a momentary state, not a fixed personality trait. All dogs showing moderate to severe fear must be flagged with team leaders, the behaviour team, and the behaviour veterinarian. Environmental changes such as partial visual barriers or a hiding crate should be implemented for fearful dogs.

Fear and anxiety present differently in every dog. Read the whole animal in context, not just one signal.

Fear and anxiety body language signs
  • BOP (Back Of Pen) — retreats to the back of the pen when approached
  • Trembling or freezing in place
  • Yawning — a calming and displacement signal, not tiredness
  • Avoidance — actively moving away from the handler
  • Disengagement — looking away to avoid the trigger
  • Vocalisation — growling, barking, or whining
  • Tense body — stiff posture, weight shifted back
  • Dilated pupils
  • Pulled commissure — lips pulled back horizontally
  • Body weight shifted back, ready to flee
  • Whale eye — whites of the eyes visible
  • Lip licking (always check context: near the food trolley this may simply be hunger, not fear)
  • Body or head held low
  • Furrowed brow
  • Tail tucked between legs
  • Ears flat or pinned back
  • Piloerection — hackles raised along the spine

Fear and anxiety body language reference. Assess the whole animal in context — no single sign is diagnostic on its own.

Defensive vs offensive aggression

When a dog displays aggression, it is critical to identify whether it is rooted in fear (defensive) or is true offensive aggression. These are distinct emotional states. A dog showing either type of aggression at FAS 5 must not be approached.

Defensive (fear-based) aggression. Body lowered, tail tucked, corner of mouth pulled back. The dog is scared and reacting to a perceived threat.

Offensive aggression. Body upright and leaning forward, tail up and stiff, direct eye contact. The dog is confident and initiating.

Defensive aggression (fear-based)
Body lowered and tense, tail tucked with little or no movement, ears flat or back, pupils dilated, nose wrinkled, lips slightly curled with teeth partially visible, corner of mouth pulled back. The dog wants distance and is reacting out of fear. This is by far the most common presentation in shelter dogs.
Offensive aggression
Body upright and leaning forward, up on toes, tail raised and stiff, ears forward, direct eye contact, hackles raised, growling or snarling. The dog is confident and initiating. This is less common in shelter dogs but must be recognised.
⚠ When NOT to approachDo not approach a dog showing FAS 5 combined with either defensive or offensive aggression. Stop the session immediately. Notify the Vet AA. These dogs require veterinary behaviour assessment and may need psychotropic medication before behaviour modification can safely begin.
Context always mattersAlways read the whole picture. A dog lip licking near the food trolley is likely hungry, not stressed. A single sign means very little. Look for multiple signs occurring together and always consider what has happened earlier in the day.

Before entering any pen or approaching any dog, take a moment to think from the dog's point of view. This is called the considered approach and it applies to every interaction.

First week in shelterDogs are particularly stressed during their first week in the shelter. Aversive handling should be minimised and all interactions during this period should focus on building trust rather than achieving training goals.
What to consider
  • Senses: what can the dog smell, hear, see, and feel right now? Is the environment loud, busy, or unpredictable?
  • History: has this dog had negative experiences with people? Has anything stressful happened earlier in the day?
  • Body language: read the dog before you touch the gate. If the dog looks tense or has retreated to BOP, slow down.
  • Your movement: calm and purposeful. No sudden gestures. Approach from the side, not face-on.
  • Your energy: slow down before you reach the pen. Loud voices, rushing, and fast movement increase fear.
Do not lure a scared dog forward with foodLuring a dog towards something it fears with food may look like progress but actually reinforces the fear and slows training overall. Wait for the dog to offer more confident behaviour, then reward it. Give the dog control over the pace of interaction.
Golden ruleNever rush a fearful dog. Any progress made slowly is progress that lasts.

The following structure is recommended for consistent progress with fearful dogs. In practice, not every shift will achieve the full program, and that is expected. Any consistent positive interaction counts.

Session structure
15 minutes per day, 5 days per week, with 2 non-consecutive rest days.
Staffing
Ideally the same 3 staff members for the BMOD phase. Consistency builds trust. Expand to additional staff gradually as the dog progresses. Start with 1 to 2 people and build the social environment slowly.
Enrichment
7 days per week regardless of BMOD sessions. See the enrichment section below and the Enrichment Volunteer Handbook.
Progress recording
Use of the fearful dog assessment +/- video records of the patient every 2 weeks until goals achieved. Record in Teams as a task comment until graduation.
Note on real-world constraintsThis program requires adequate staffing. If full sessions are not possible, prioritise enrichment delivery and brief positive interactions over the prescribed structure. Something consistent is always better than nothing.

Distance testing identifies how close a handler can be before the dog shows signs of fear. Always begin behind a physical barrier such as a glass door, baby gate, or closed pen.

ObjectiveIdentify the distance at which a handler can be present before reaching the dog's threshold. Build a foundation of trust using DS/CC within the dog's comfort zone, gradually reducing that distance over time.
#Step
1Walk past and observe
Walk past the dog's pen at a comfortable distance. Observe the dog's body language and assess their FAS level. For extremely fearful dogs, maintain maximum distance.
2Toss treats and move on
Walk past the pen, say hello in a calm voice, toss a small handful of high-value treats to the back of the pen, and continue walking. Note whether the dog eats the treats. Repeat this step across multiple sessions.
3Establish eating in your presence
Once the dog is eating the tossed treats, crouch down facing away from the dog, toss 5 small pieces of food to the back of the pen, then wait for 5 seconds. If the dog approaches or eats, toss a little more food then calmly stand and walk on.
4Move treats progressively closer to you
Crouch sideways to the dog and avoid eye contact. Toss treats progressively closer to the front of the pen.
5Treats close to the front
If the dog is consistently taking treats in your presence, toss treats around the pen and toward the front. Only reduce distance if the dog is NOT showing increased fear signs. This step may take 30 seconds or several sessions across multiple days.
6Hand delivery through the gate
See if the dog will take a treat directly from your hand through the gate. Maintain non-threatening body language throughout.
7Enter the pen
If the dog takes treats from your hand at the gate, enter the pen. Crouch sideways to the dog and stay as far away as possible. Toss treats around the pen. Allow the dog to approach at their own pace.
8Build in-pen sessions
Continue in-pen sessions. If the dog is freely eating treats, toss them progressively closer to yourself over repeated sessions.
9Progress to consent testing
Once the dog is consistently taking treats from your hand inside the pen, they can progress to the consent testing protocol.
If the dog is not eating treatsTry a variety of high-value options: cheese, chicken, sausage, liver treats. If no treats are accepted at any level, this may indicate a very high FAS score. Consult the Vet AA immediately as psychotropic medication may be required before training can proceed.
If not progressingGo back a step and restart from where the dog was last successful. Never push through a step the dog is not ready for.

Consent testing establishes which body areas the dog is comfortable with and which it prefers to avoid. It forms the foundation of all further handling.

⚠ Note on voluntary approachIdeally, consent testing begins when the dog approaches the handler voluntarily. However, some very fearful dogs may not approach at all in the early stages. Do not proceed with consent testing until distance testing is fully complete and the dog is no longer showing an increase in FAS signs when the handler is present. If the dog is still actively retreating or vocalising, return to distance testing.
#Step
1Wait for a calm response
Ensure distance testing is complete. The dog should be able to remain near the gate without retreating when the handler is present.
2Offer initial contact
When the dog approaches or is near the gate calmly, offer a gentle touch around the chest area for 2 to 3 seconds, then stop.
3Read the consent signal
Dog leans in, nudges, or moves closer: consent to continue. Dog stiffens, lip licks, yawns, or steps back: stop immediately and give space.
4Identify comfortable areas
Work systematically through body areas. Document findings and share with the whole team.
Body area progression
Easy (start here): chest, neck, back • Moderate: tummy, legs • Difficult (only when consistently comfortable): tail, paws, head including eyes, ears, and mouth
Note: this order varies per individual dog based on history and current health status.
Document what you findRecord which areas the dog is comfortable with and which it avoids. Share this with the whole team so every person interacts consistently.

Desensitisation and counter-conditioning for touch helps a fearful dog learn that physical contact is safe. Always work below the dog's threshold.

Desensitisation (DS)
Gradual, controlled exposure to a trigger at low intensity until the trigger no longer produces a fearful response. Must be done in small, manageable increments.
Counter-conditioning (CC)
Changing the dog's emotional response to a stimulus from negative to positive by pairing it with something the dog loves, such as high-value food. If done incorrectly, CC can increase fear rather than reduce it.
⚠ Always work below thresholdStop at the first sign of discomfort. If you see any avoidance behaviour, you have moved too fast. Go back one step.
#Step
1Start at the most comfortable area
Touch briefly at the area the dog is most comfortable with (usually chest or neck) while feeding a treat simultaneously.
2Classical pairing
As the dog finishes eating, stop touching. End of repetition. Repeat until the dog is relaxed during touch and actively looks for the treat.
3Operant CC
Touch briefly, then reward AFTER stopping. The dog should now remain calm without a simultaneous treat.
4Increase duration and area
Gradually extend the duration of touch and move toward less comfortable body areas, one small step at a time.
5Finish on a positive response
If the dog shows any discomfort, go back one step. Always end the session while the dog is comfortable.
Stop immediately if you see
  • Ears back or flat
  • Lip licking or yawning
  • Whale eye
  • Gaze aversion
  • Tense body
  • Moving away
  • Tail tucked
Session length5 to 10 minutes maximum. Two short successful sessions outperform one long stressful one.

The slip lead must never be rushed with a fearful dog. Distance testing, consent testing, and some tolerance of touch around the neck must all be established first.

This dog is showing clear fear signs. Do not rush lead placement. Build trust first.

⚠ PrerequisitesDistance testing complete. Dog approaches handler voluntarily or remains calm at close proximity. Some tolerance of touch around the neck has been established.
#Step
1Introduce the lead
Show the dog the slip lead. When the dog sniffs it calmly, mark YES and reward. Repeat until calm.
2Floor exploration
Let the dog interact with the lead on the floor. Reward calm investigation.
3Loop near nose level
Hold the lead loop open near the dog's nose level, not above the head. Reward the dog for approaching.
4Voluntary head through
Over several sessions, gradually lower the loop so the dog can move their head through it voluntarily while eating a treat.
5Build tolerance
Do not tighten the lead until the dog is fully comfortable with it resting loosely over their head. Sessions maximum 10 minutes.
⚠ Never force the lead over a fearful dog's headThis creates a strong negative association with leads and handlers and significantly delays progress.
Clip lead alternative: dogs who are too fearful for a slip lead over the headIf a dog is too fearful to accept a slip lead placed over the head, and the dog is already wearing a collar, a clip lead can be used as an alternative. Begin by practising bringing the clip close to the collar, then clip and unclip it calmly, pairing each repetition with a high-value treat. Gradually build the dog's tolerance through repeated, low-pressure exposures until clipping is reliably calm. This systematic desensitisation to the clip mechanism allows the dog to associate the lead with positive outcomes before any restraint or movement is introduced.

The LOOK cue teaches the dog to focus on the handler's eyes. It builds focus and confidence, and is useful when a trigger cannot be avoided.

LOOK cue: hold your finger in front of your eye. Dog makes eye contact. Mark YES and reward immediately.

Equipment
  • Treat pouch
  • High-value treats (chicken, beef, soft cheese)
#Step
1Lure with treat near face
With a treat in hand, hold it to the side of your face. Dog follows the treat upward.
2Mark the look
As soon as the dog looks at the treat held near your face, mark YES and reward.
3Fade the treat
Gradually move the treat in front of your eye. Wait for brief eye contact, then mark YES and reward.
4Remove treat from hand
Hold your finger in front of your eye with no treat. Dog looks at your eye, mark YES and reward with the other hand.
5Add the cue
Say LOOK and give the hand signal (finger pointed at eye) just before the behaviour.
6Extend duration
Hold eye contact a few seconds longer before marking YES. Build gradually.
When to use LOOKAt the gate before entering. During handling if the dog becomes overwhelmed. On walks when a trigger appears in the environment.

Touch training teaches the dog to press their nose to the handler's hand on cue. It redirects focus to the handler and is useful when the dog is overwhelmed by the environment.

TOUCH cue: present an open palm. Dog touches nose to your hand. Mark YES and reward.

#Step
1Present closed fist
Place a treat in your closed fist and hold it in front of the dog's nose, about 10 cm away.
2Mark the nose touch
When the dog's nose touches your hand, mark YES and drop the treat on the floor as a reward.
3Repeat until consistent
Repeat until the dog consistently touches their nose to your hand on presentation.
4Remove treat from fist
Present an open hand with no treat. Dog touches nose to palm, mark YES, reward with the other hand.
5Add the cue
Say TOUCH just before presenting your hand. Vary the position: lower, higher, left, right, swap hands.
Using TOUCH in practiceA frightened dog will often respond to TOUCH even when too anxious for other cues. It gives the dog something to do and redirects attention to you.

Enrichment is not optional for fearful dogs. It reduces baseline stress, builds positive associations with the shelter environment, and gives the dog a sense of control.

Enrichment priorities
  • Lick mats and kongs: place outside or at the front of the pen first. The dog can engage without the stress of human proximity. Gradually move closer as the dog relaxes.
  • Scent enrichment: sheep's wool, soiled straw, or dried lavender near the bedding. Sniffing is calming and self-directed.
  • Quiet presence: a calm person sitting near the pen and reading aloud helps fearful dogs habituate to human presence without pressure. See Books and Calm Sunday in the Enrichment Handbook.
  • Busy boxes: foraging opportunities that do not require human interaction.
  • Adaptil spray: apply to bedding, not directly on the dog. Leave 10 minutes before placing bedding in the pen.
See the Enrichment Volunteer Handbook for full setup guides for each enrichment type.
⚠ What is flooding?Flooding means forced, constant exposure to a fear-inducing stimulus with no means of escape. It is very stressful, commonly increases the level of fear, and is not recommended under any circumstances. It can result in a bite with no warning.
  • Do not reach over or above a fearful dog's head to pat them
  • Do not stare directly into a fearful dog's eyes
  • Do not force a slip lead over the head of a fearful dog
  • Do not rush any step in this guide
  • Do not lure a scared dog forward with food toward something it fears
  • Do not continue a session when the dog cannot take treats or is showing avoidance
  • Do not punish any fear responses including growling, barking, or retreating
  • Do not have multiple unfamiliar people interact with a fearful dog at the same time

Growling and retreating are communication. Removing these signals through punishment does not remove the fear. It removes the warning and increases bite risk without notice.
