Cooperative care

Handling and treatment training

RSPCA Victoria Behaviour Program • For shelter staff and veterinary team

How to use this guide

Every protocol in this guide begins with consent testing. Do not skip it. Session duration is 5 to 10 minutes, 5 days per week with 2 rest days. Always use high-value treats. Tap any section to expand.

DS/CC at a glanceDesensitisation (DS): gradual, controlled exposure to a stimulus at low intensity until it no longer produces a fearful response. Counter-conditioning (CC): changing the emotional response from negative to positive by pairing the stimulus with something the dog loves. If done incorrectly, CC can increase fear. Always work below the dog's threshold.

Protocols

Consent testing is the gateway to all handling. It identifies which body areas the dog is comfortable with before any treatment begins. Complete this before every new protocol and with every new handler.

Comfortable body language: continue
  • Dog nudges head or body into your hand
  • Dog moves closer or leans in
  • Soft eyes, face, body, and tail
Avoidance and discomfort: stop immediately
  • Ears back
  • Lip licking or yawning
  • Whale eye
  • Gaze aversion
  • Tense body
  • Moving away
  • Low or tucked tail
  • Trembling
#Step
1Approach calmly
Consider the animal's point of view: what can they see, hear, smell, and feel right now? Begin at a distance that does not trigger avoidance.
2Offer touch at comfortable areas
Start at the chin, neck, chest, or shoulders. Offer gentle touch and observe the response immediately.
3Read the response continuously
If the dog leans into your hand, they are comfortable. If they show any avoidance sign, stop immediately.
4Explore systematically
Work gradually through different body areas. Document findings and share with the whole team.
Body area progression
Easy: chest, neck, back • Moderate: tummy, legs • Difficult: tail, paws, head including eyes, ears, and mouth
Order varies per dog based on history and current health status.

Muzzles are a welfare tool, not a punishment. A well-fitted, well-trained basket muzzle protects the dog, the handler, and the veterinary team. Training should be completed before it is needed urgently.

Muzzle types. Basket muzzles (centre and right) are preferred as they allow the dog to pant, drink, and accept treats.

Selecting a muzzle

Choose a basket muzzle where possible. It allows the dog to pant, drink, and accept treats during the conditioning process. Match the size to the dog's snout shape and length.

#Step
1Introduce the muzzle
Show the dog the muzzle. When the dog sniffs it calmly, mark YES and reward. Repeat until the dog sniffs the muzzle with relaxed body language.
2Feed through the basket
Place treats inside the muzzle and allow the dog to eat from it. Do not put it on yet. Cup your hand underneath to catch treats, or smear peanut butter or liverwurst inside.
3Nose in voluntarily
Once the dog is eagerly placing their nose into the muzzle to reach treats, progress to the neck strap.
4Introduce the neck strap
With the dog's nose in the muzzle eating, briefly clip the neck strap, then immediately unclip. Repeat until the dog is relaxed with the click sound.
5Build duration
Gradually leave the strap clipped for 5 seconds, then 10, then 30. Continue pushing treats through the basket holes throughout. Frozen food in the tip of the muzzle extends wearing time.
6Practice foundation behaviours
Practice Sit and Touch while the muzzle is on. Reward by pushing treats through the basket. This normalises the muzzle as part of regular training.
Never rush muzzle trainingA dog that has been forcibly muzzled will strongly resist future muzzle training. Take several sessions to build a positive association before any urgency arises.

Overall goal: for the dog to feel safe during eye examination and medication delivery through gradual positive exposure. Train on the non-painful eye first where possible.

Left to right: chin rest position, two-hand eyelid method, single-hand method.

#Step
1Consent testing
Complete consent testing. Establish comfortable touch around the face and head before proceeding.
2Classical CC: chin rest and facial handling
Use treats to lure the dog to rest their chin on your lap or your hand. Left-handed handler: dog rests chin on the left side. Right-handed: right side. Feed treats while gently rubbing around the eyes for 3 to 5 seconds. Stop touching as the treats run out. Goal: dog tolerates gentle petting around the eyes while receiving treats.
3Operant CC: eyelid handling
Gently pull the upper eyelid up and lower eyelid down using the thumb and index finger of the non-dominant hand (two-hand method). Alternatively, lift the upper lid with the palm edge near the little finger (one-hand method). Hold 3 to 5 seconds, release, then immediately mark YES and reward. Goal: dog allows eyelid handling for 3 to 5 seconds without reacting.
4Introduce the medication
Show the dog the eye medication bottle. When they sniff it, mark YES and reward. If the dog shows fear of the bottle, place it near treats at a distance and gradually move it closer over sessions. Goal: dog does not avoid the presence of the medication bottle.
5In-pen training and practice administration
Repeat all above steps in the pen. If arousal or fear increases in the pen, use an outside area instead. Use eye lubricant for practice runs. Pre-squeeze a rice-grain amount of ointment before approaching. Apply lubricant to both eyes. Reward each administration.
6Ongoing maintenance
Continue rewarding each administration. Incorporate enrichment such as play or a lick mat at the start of each session to build positive associations with treatment time. Consistency prevents regression.
Training tipIncorporate the LOOK cue during eye treatment sessions. It focuses the dog's attention, keeps the head still, and builds a predictable routine for the dog.

Overall goal: for the dog to feel safe during ear examination and medication delivery. Train on the non-painful ear first. If the dog reacts strongly at the painful ear due to pain rather than fear, stop immediately and inform the vet.

#Step
1Consent testing
Complete consent testing. Establish comfortable touch around the neck and back of the ears before proceeding.
2Classical CC
Rub the skin or fur near the back of the ear while feeding treats continuously for 3 to 5 seconds. Stop rubbing as the dog finishes eating. Goal: dog accepts a finger placed near the ear while receiving treats.
3Operant CC
Rub around the ear and stop just before the dog becomes irritated. Mark YES and reward for holding still. For dogs with long ears, practice flipping the ear open for visual examination. Goal: dog tolerates a finger placed in the ear for several seconds without reacting.
4Vet environment familiarisation
Visit the examination room, yard, or behaviour assessment area with enjoyable activities: food, snuffle mat, play. Create positive associations with the clinical environment before treatment begins.
5Restraint and syringe introduction
One handler restrains the dog while another inserts a finger into the ear briefly, then rewards. Progress to a 1 mL syringe for dogs under 10 kg, or 3 mL syringe for dogs over 10 kg. Gently insert the syringe tip into the ear canal to mimic medication. Do not squirt anything during practice. Goal: dog allows one handler to hold the head while another inserts the syringe for several seconds.
6Actual treatment
Use Adaptil spray or anxiolytic medication if indicated. Provide high-value treats throughout. Use slow, gentle technique. Ensure the lead is present and relaxed. Continue to reward after each treatment session to prevent regression.
Note on painful earsA dog with an active ear infection may react strongly at early steps due to pain, not fear. If this occurs, stop the session, document the response, and inform the vet. Pain management may be required before behaviour training can progress.

Overall goal: for the dog to feel safe during injection procedures. Particularly useful for dogs on long-term injectable medications or with a history of needle phobia.

#Step
1Consent testing
Complete consent testing. Establish comfortable touch around the shoulder blades and neck scruff before proceeding.
2Classical CC
Rub the skin near the shoulder blades while feeding treats continuously for 3 to 5 seconds. Stop as the treats finish. Goal: dog allows firm but gentle grasping of the skin at the back of the neck for several seconds while receiving treats.
3Operant CC
Rub around the neck scruff and stop just before the dog becomes irritated. Mark YES and reward for holding still. Goal: dog allows firm neck scruff handling for several seconds without treats present.
4Introduce the syringe
Show the dog the syringe (no needle). When they sniff it, mark YES and reward. If the dog avoids the syringe, place it near the treat bowl and gradually move it closer over sessions. Goal: dog is comfortable with the syringe being present.
5Restraint and simulated needle
Hold the dog in the injection position with the lead present and reward. Use a toothpick or pen cap to gently press on the neck scruff area, simulating the needle sensation. Mark YES and reward. Have a second handler administer the mock needle while you feed treats from the front. For dogs anxious about someone leaning over them, administer from the side or behind and consider a light towel over the head.
6Actual injection
Use Adaptil spray as a calming aid. Provide high-value treats throughout. Vet uses slow, gentle technique. Reward immediately after each injection. Maintain ongoing counter-conditioning after every subsequent injection to prevent anticipatory fear from developing.
Reducing anticipatory fearDogs that receive regular injections often develop anticipatory fear of the syringe, the room, or even being caught. Ongoing counter-conditioning after every injection session prevents this from escalating over time.
