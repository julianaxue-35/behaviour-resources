Enrichment Volunteer Handbook

RSPCA Victoria • Behaviour Program • For enrichment volunteer use

Welcome

Thank you for being part of our enrichment team. Our goal is simple: every dog in the shelter receives some form of enrichment every day. Your work directly supports the wellbeing and behaviour of dogs in our care, and makes a real difference to their time with us.

This handbook tells you everything you need to know to get started.

Before you start

Check in with the Vet AA on duty at the beginning of every shift. They will let you know which dogs are a priority and what needs to be done that day.

Distribute enrichment outside of feeding and cleaning times to avoid overcrowding in the pods.

Targeted dogs

Work through the following order each shift. The goal is that every dog gets something every day.

  1. Behaviour program dogs
    Identified by a red medication treatment sheet on the kennel clipboard. Distribute enrichment directly to these dogs.
  2. Quarantine and isolation dogs
    Prepare enrichment items and leave them in the enrichment tub. Animal attendants will dispense these daily. Do not enter quarantine or isolation pens.
  3. All other pods
    If time allows, prepare and distribute extra enrichment items to dogs in the remaining pods.

A red treatment sheet on the kennel clipboard means this dog is in the behaviour program.

⚠ Staff Only dogs Do not enter a pen marked "Staff Only". Enrichment must be delivered by an animal attendant. If the dog is in a double pen, you may enter the empty side only if you know how to safely operate the guillotine doors. Isolate the dog, place the item in the empty side, exit and lock the pen, then open the guillotine. When in doubt, ask staff.

Dogs shred, dig, and forage through a box of paper and treats. Cardboard enrichment is low-cost, high-value, and perfect for busy dogs.

Items required

  • Cardboard box (any size)
  • Scrunched newspaper or paper
  • Kibble or small treats
  • Optional: sheep's wool or soiled straw for added scent

Steps

  1. Fill the box with scrunched newspaper, small cardboard tubes, and kibble hidden throughout.
  2. Optional: tuck in a scent item such as sheep's wool or soiled straw.
  3. Place the box in the pen and step back. Let the dog explore at their own pace.

Cardboard shredding is natural, calming behaviour.

⚠ Observe at all timesStay and monitor the dog. Remove any pieces large enough to be swallowed.

Licking releases calming hormones and gives anxious dogs a focused, positive activity. Kongs and lick mats are among the most effective tools we use.

Kong — items required

  • Large or medium kong (not small or extra-small — choking hazard for large dogs)
  • Royal Canin Gastrointestinal kibble
  • Anko Meal Topper Beef Gravy sachet
  • Peanut butter (xylitol-free only)

Kong — steps

  1. Mix one yellow scoop of kibble with one sachet of Anko Gravy (or 1 tablespoon of wet food).
  2. Fill the kong with the mixture.
  3. Seal the small end with a thin scrape of peanut butter.
  4. Kongs can be stored in the freezer until needed, or frozen overnight for Wednesdays.
📷 Photo to add: Filled kong sealed at the small end with peanut butter

Lick mat — items required

  • Lick mat
  • Peanut butter (xylitol-free) and/or wet Royal Canin Gastrointestinal food

Lick mat — steps

  1. Smear the mat generously with peanut butter, wet food, or a mix of both.
  2. If the mat has suction cups, stick it to the inside wall of the pen.
  3. Alternatively, use a carabiner to attach the mat or an ice cream container lid to the pen mesh so the dog licks through.
📷 Photo to add: Lick mat smeared with food, attached to pen wall or mesh
⚠ Before giving any treatsCheck the observation chart. Do not give treats to dogs with recent vomiting or diarrhoea. Never use xylitol-containing peanut butter.

Frozen enrichment extends treat time and is especially helpful on warm days. Prepare trays the afternoon or evening before.

Frozen muffin trays — items required

  • Muffin tin or silicone ice mould
  • Royal Canin Gastrointestinal kibble
  • Anko Meal Topper Beef Gravy sachet
  • 1 cup water
  • Sheet of cardboard (to place under trays)

Steps

  1. Sprinkle a single layer of kibble into each section of the tray.
  2. Dilute one sachet of Anko Gravy in one cup of water and pour over the kibble.
  3. Place a sheet of cardboard under the trays to keep them rigid and stop them freezing together.
  4. Freeze overnight. Check they are ready at the start of your shift.

Muffin trays filled with kibble and gravy, ready to freeze.

Frozen kongs: kongs prepared on Tuesdays can be frozen overnight and distributed on Wednesdays for extra difficulty.

⚠ Check observation chart firstDo not give to dogs with recent vomiting or diarrhoea.

Sniffing is one of the most mentally tiring and calming activities for dogs. Vet AAs run formal nosework sessions on Thursdays. You can set up in-pen scent enrichment alongside those sessions.

Volunteer vs. formal noseworkVet AA nosework involves trained hides and tracking. Your version is enrichment only. No training required — just set it up and let the dog explore.

Snuffle mat — items required

  • Snuffle mat (large grey cardboard box, ground floor outside yellow pod)
  • Kibble or small liver treats

Steps

  1. Tuck kibble or liver treats between the fabric strips of the snuffle mat.
  2. Place the mat in the pen and let the dog search.
  3. Supervise closely throughout. Remove the mat when the dog has finished.

Stay close. Remove the mat once the dog finishes searching.

⚠ Observe at all timesClose observation is required to prevent the dog from ingesting the mat or any foreign material. This applies to all enrichment objects and toys.

Scent cardboard tubes — items required

  • Cardboard tube
  • Soiled straw from the barn or small animals clinic, or sheep's wool from the Behaviour kitchen

Steps

  1. Fold in one end of the cardboard tube.
  2. Loosely fill with soiled straw or sheep's wool.
  3. Fold in the other end to seal.
  4. Place in the pen. The dog will sniff and investigate the novel scent.
📷 Photo to add: Cardboard tube packed with straws or natural material as scent enrichment
⚠ Observe at all timesMonitor the dog and remove the tube if they attempt to ingest any material.

Foraging means searching for food hidden inside a simple item. It keeps dogs mentally active and taps into natural seeking behaviour.

Treat tubes — items required

  • Cardboard tube (paper towel or toilet roll)
  • Kibble or small treats

Steps

  1. Fold in one end of the tube.
  2. Fill with kibble or treats.
  3. Fold in the other end to seal.
  4. Place in the pen. The dog will roll and chew the tube to reach the treats.

Fold both ends firmly. Drop one or more tubes in the pen.

Towel foraging — items required

  • Bath towel
  • Kibble or liver treats

Steps

  1. Lay the towel flat on the pen floor and sprinkle treats evenly across it.
  2. Beginners: fold the towel loosely once so treats are easy to find.
  3. Experienced dogs: roll the towel tightly, or loosely tie it for a greater challenge.

Roll tighter for dogs that find it quickly.

⚠ Observe at all timesStay with the dog and remove the towel when they have finished foraging.

Saturday focuses on slowing things down: slower eating, calmer behaviour, and positive interactions. Two activities below — try both if you have time.

Puzzle feeders — items required

  • Puzzle feeder toy
  • Royal Canin Gastrointestinal kibble

Steps

  1. Load the puzzle feeder with kibble at the appropriate difficulty level.
  2. Place it in the pen.
  3. Supervise throughout. Remove when the dog finishes or loses interest.

Puzzle feeders slow the pace of eating and provide mental stimulation.

⚠ Observe at all timesDo not leave a dog alone with a puzzle feeder. Remove when done.

YES for Calm exercise — items required

  • Small, high-value treats (something extra tasty)

What it teaches

When you arrive at a pod, dogs are often jumping and barking. This exercise teaches them that being calm is what gets the reward — not jumping.

Steps

  1. Approach the pen. Do not enter. Do not speak to the dog yet.
  2. Wait quietly outside the pen while the dog jumps and barks. Ignore this behaviour completely.
  3. The moment the dog stops jumping or barking — even for a second — say "YES" in a clear, upbeat voice.
  4. Immediately toss a treat through the pen mesh or gate into the pen.
  5. Repeat for a few minutes per dog.
  6. If the dog cannot settle, move on and try again next time. Never enter the pen for this exercise.
Key ruleOnly reward the calm moment. Ignoring the jumping and barking is just as important as rewarding the quiet.

Reading aloud to a shelter dog helps them become comfortable with calm human presence. It is especially valuable for fearful dogs and for volunteers who are building their confidence with handling.

⚠ TimingRun this activity outside of feeding and cleaning hours. Dogs are noisier during those times and the activity will be less effective.

Which dogs to choose

Ask the Vet AA or animal attendant on duty to help identify suitable dogs. Fearful dogs that are wary of people benefit most. Avoid starting with highly aroused dogs that bark constantly — ten minutes of a dog barking at you is not enrichment for either of you.

Once dogs have learned the YES for Calm exercise (Saturdays), more aroused dogs can also begin to benefit from reading sessions as they learn to self-regulate.

Items required

  • Any book (fiction, non-fiction — whatever you enjoy)

Steps

  1. Sit in a comfortable position near the kennel door or gate.
  2. Open your book and begin reading aloud in a slow, calm, gentle voice.
  3. You do not need to make eye contact with the dog. Read naturally.
  4. If the dog approaches the gate, let them sniff and investigate at their own pace.
  5. Do not reach in or force interaction. Your calm presence is the enrichment.
  6. Aim for 10 to 15 minutes per dog.
  7. For Staff Only dogs: sit outside the pen door only. Do not enter.
📷 Photo to add: Volunteer sitting near a kennel reading a book to a dog
Why it worksHearing a calm human voice regularly and without pressure helps shelter dogs associate people with safety. Research published in Animals (2021) found measurably reduced stress behaviours in shelter dogs during reading sessions.
