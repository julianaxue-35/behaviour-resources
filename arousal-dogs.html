Arousal dogs

Recognition and behaviour modification

RSPCA Victoria Behaviour Program • For shelter staff and volunteers

How to use this guide

Arousal dogs are not bad dogs. They are dogs that have not yet learned to regulate their emotions in a highly stimulating shelter environment. Consistency, predictability, and patience are the three most important tools you have.

Key principleA dog that cannot take treats or respond to known cues is over threshold. Do not push through. Return at a quieter time.

Canine Arousal Scale

Score the dog before each session and record the level in your session note below. Use this scale to guide your handling approach and to track progress over time.

🟢 Neutral — H
BodyRelaxed body; soft gaze; neutral or low tail wag; relaxed ears.
BehaviourCalm; gentle with treats; responsive to cues; easily breaks focus from a stimulus.
HandlingNo defensive handling needed.
🟡 Mild arousal — H/TR
BodyBody may be tense; ears shifting forward or lateral; momentary stare; dilated pupils; may pant; tail above neutral.
BehaviourOccasional barking, whining, pacing, jumping; may mouth lightly with no bruising or broken skin; may be snatchy with food. Easily breaks focus with verbal cues and treats.
HandlingEasily interrupted or redirected with treats or cues. May need brief defensive handling.
🟠 Moderate arousal — TR/TM
BodyTense body; high rapid tail wag; direct stare; panting; dilated pupils; may show piloerection.
BehaviourRepetitive barking, pacing, jumping or wall rebounding; moderate mouthing with minor abrasions or bruising; intense treat-taking with teeth making contact with hands. Difficult to distract despite treats or cues when the trigger is present.
HandlingConsistent defensive handling required. Inform kennel team leaders and Vet AA. Capture behaviour on video where possible. Chill protocol may be required. Team leaders to decide on appropriate staff and volunteer handling.
🔴 Severe arousal — TM/UU
BodyRigid body; rapid tail wag or flagging high tail; fixed intense stare unable to break; excessive panting; piloerection.
BehaviourRepetitive barking or high-pitched squealing; forceful contact with kennel walls; severe mouthing with punctures or deep bruising; indiscriminately pursues or lunges toward a stimulus without regard for own safety.
HandlingCannot be interrupted or redirected. Seized or experienced staff only. Inform kennel team leaders and Vet AA immediately. Capture behaviour on video. Chill protocol will likely be required. A management plan must be in place to mitigate bite risk.
Tips for useArousal reflects a momentary state, not a fixed personality trait. Mild to moderate arousal must be escalated to team leaders and Vet AAs for environmental management. Moderate arousal should also be flagged with the behaviour team and behaviour veterinarian for potential psychotropic medication. Severe arousal dogs must be handled by seized or experienced staff only.

Sections

Arousal is a heightened emotional and physical state. In the shelter environment it is very common and does not mean a dog is dangerous.

Arousal signs reference chart. Look for clusters of signs, not just one in isolation.

Common shelter arousal signs
  • Pacing, circling, spinning, or bouncing in the kennel
  • Sustained barking at the gate when approached
  • Jumping on the gate repeatedly (height seeking)
  • Mouthing or play-biting hands, arms, or clothing
  • Grabbing, tugging, or mouthing the leash
  • Pulling hard on leash with difficulty redirecting
  • Snatching treats (unable to take food gently)
  • Unable to sustain focus on a cue before redirecting
  • Nipping at clothing when walking past
  • Dilated pupils, constant panting, furrowed brow
  • Penis crowning (intact males: sign of extreme arousal)
Impact on welfare and adoptabilityHighly aroused dogs often become Staff Only, receive fewer walks, and have longer shelter stays as a result. Reducing arousal through training and enrichment directly improves adoption outcomes.

Before behaviour modification, identify what is triggering the arousal. The same intervention does not work for all triggers.

Noise and cleaning
Pressure hoses, cleaning equipment, and loud activity are time-based triggers. Schedule training at quieter times.
People
New handlers, visitors, or busy periods. Work on distance and positive associations with people first.
Other dogs
Nearby dogs barking or visual access to other dogs. Physical barriers and visual blocks help. Go Find training is very effective here.
Feeding and cleaning times
Arousal peaks predictably at these times. Do not attempt slip lead or training during peak arousal periods.
High traffic
Multiple staff moving through the pod simultaneously. Quieter periods are significantly more productive for training.

The single most effective strategy for reducing baseline arousal is predictability. Dogs that know what to expect, when, and from whom will have measurably lower arousal across the day.

  • Same staff, same sequence, same cues for feeding, cleaning, and walking.
  • Prepare enrichment before entering the pod. A dog that already has a lick mat or kong has a lower arousal peak when you arrive.
  • Approach the pod calmly. A loud, fast entry raises every dog's arousal simultaneously.
  • Walk the row tossing treats for 4 feet on the floor before targeting any individual dog.
  • Announce yourself before opening gates.
Consistent routine reduces arousal better than any single training session.If only one thing from this guide is implemented, make it this.

How you approach and enter the pen sets the tone for the entire interaction. YES for Calm is the foundation of all work with arousal dogs and must be used at every pen, every time.

Do not enter while the dog is jumping at the gate. Wait for 4 paws on the floor first.

Step-by-step: before entering and YES for Calm
#Step
1Walk the row first
As you approach the pod, walk the row tossing a few treats into any pen where the dog has 4 paws on the floor. This raises the reward rate for calm behaviour across the whole pod before you target any individual dog.
2Approach the target pen with treats ready
Have high-value treats in hand before you reach the gate. Treats must be immediately accessible.
3Wait for 4 paws on the floor
If the dog is jumping at the gate, do not toss treats yet. Wait quietly. Ignore the jumping and barking completely. Do not look at, speak to, or reach toward the dog while it is jumping.
4Mark YES the moment jumping stops
The instant all 4 paws are on the floor, even for just a second, say YES in a clear upbeat voice and immediately toss a treat into the pen.
5Repeat before entry
Repeat the YES for Calm sequence 3 to 5 times before entering. Cue SIT and reward 2 to 3 times if the dog is able to respond.
6Enter the pen
Open the gate calmly. If height-seeking resumes after entry, turn your back, wait for 4 paws on the floor, and mark YES again before continuing.
Key ruleIgnoring the jumping and barking is just as important as marking the calm. If you look at, speak to, or reach toward the dog while it is jumping, you reward the arousal. Consistency from every team member is essential.
If the dog cannot settle at allSome dogs are too aroused at certain times to engage. Note the trigger and return during a quieter period.

Place the slip lead only after the dog has 4 paws on the floor and can respond to SIT. The dog's arousal peaks at three stages: when you appear outside the pen, when you enter, and when you produce the lead. Reduce arousal at each stage before moving forward.

#Step
1Walk the row
Walk the row tossing treats for calm 4 paws on floor in each pen before approaching the target dog.
2YES for Calm at the gate
Approach the target pen. Wait for 4 paws on the floor. Mark YES and toss a treat. Repeat 3 to 5 times. Cue SIT and reward before touching the gate.
3Enter calmly
Enter with treats in hand. If the dog jumps again, turn your back, wait for 4 paws, mark YES, then continue.
4Cue SIT before lead
When the dog is settled and responding to SIT, hold the slip lead loop open and lure the dog's head through with a treat.
5Head down technique
If the dog is still moving, toss a treat to the floor. While the dog's head is down eating, gently place the slip lead loop over the head.
6Calm exit
Once the lead is on, wait for a moment of calm before moving toward the gate. Mark YES and reward before exiting.

Sit is one of the most useful cues for managing arousal. A sitting dog cannot simultaneously be jumping. Use it at every natural transition point.

SIT hand signal: palm facing up, raise the hand. Use the same signal as the rest of the team.

#Step
1Lure into position
Hold a treat at the dog's nose. Slowly move it up and slightly back over the head. The nose follows and the back end lowers.
2Mark the sit
As soon as the bottom touches the floor, mark YES and reward.
3Fade the lure
Use the hand signal (palm up, raise hand) without a treat in the hand. Mark YES when the dog sits, reward from the other hand.
4Add the verbal cue
Say SIT just before the hand signal. Repeat until the dog responds to the verbal cue alone.

Touch redirects an aroused dog's focus onto the handler. It is particularly useful for dogs that are easily distracted or beginning to fixate on environmental triggers.

TOUCH cue: present an open palm. Dog touches nose to your hand. Mark YES and reward.

#Step
1Present closed fist
Place a treat in your closed fist. Hold it in front of the dog's nose about 10 cm away.
2Mark the nose touch
When the dog's nose touches your hand, mark YES and drop the treat on the floor as a reward.
3Remove treat from fist
Present an open palm with no treat. Dog touches nose to palm, mark YES, reward with the other hand.
4Add the cue
Say TOUCH just before presenting your hand. Vary the position: lower, higher, left, right, swap hands.

LOOK teaches the dog to focus on the handler's eyes. Use it before the dog has a chance to fixate on a trigger.

LOOK cue: point your finger at your eye. Use this before the dog fixates on a trigger.

#Step
1Lure with treat near face
With a treat in hand, hold it to the side of your face. Dog follows the treat.
2Mark the look
As soon as the dog looks at the treat near your face, mark YES and reward.
3Move treat to eye level
Gradually move the treat in front of your eye. Wait for brief eye contact, mark YES and reward.
4Remove treat from hand
Finger in front of eye with no treat. Dog makes eye contact, mark YES and reward with the other hand.
5Add the cue
Say LOOK and point finger at eye just before the behaviour.

Go Find activates the dog's natural seeking drive, which is physiologically incompatible with peak arousal. It is one of the fastest de-escalation tools available in the shelter environment.

When to use Go Find
  • To settle a dog too excited to respond to other cues
  • To redirect jumping or mouthing onto floor-level sniffing
  • When another dog is visible and triggering reactivity
  • As a calm transition before or after a walk
#Step
1Get attention
Hold a treat in your hand and wait for the dog to look at you briefly.
2Scatter and cue
Toss the treat away from yourself and say GO FIND at the same time.
3Repeat as dog returns
As the dog turns back toward you, show another treat and repeat.
4Modulate the pace
Start fast while the dog is at peak arousal. Slow down as the arousal decreases.
5If dog cannot settle
Cue SIT or TOUCH first, then proceed with Go Find. If no cue is working at all, end the session and return later.
The seeking systemEngaging the seeking drive through sniffing and searching is calming. A dog with its nose to the floor is physiologically unable to maintain peak arousal. (Dog Smart Training and Behaviour, 2025)

Leash and handling principles
  • Short leash, never tight. Short gives more response time. Tight increases arousal.
  • Limbs in your centreline. Outstretched arms give the dog more to grab.
  • Toss treats, do not hand-deliver to mouthy or aroused dogs. Teaches the dog to look down, not at your hand.
  • Situational awareness. Know where other dogs, handlers, and exits are at all times.
  • Never forcefully remove food or toys. Use trade-outs and management.
Leash lock: thumb lock

Loop the lead around your thumb and close your fist over it. Far stronger than open-finger grip. Use for any unknown or aroused dog.

Leash lock: index finger and thumb lock

Loop around both index finger and thumb, close your fist. Use for stronger dogs or when extra security is needed.

Double leash technique
#Step
1Clip second leash to slip lead ring
Attach a second leash to the metal ring on the slip lead. Note: does not work on all-fabric slip leads without a metal ring.
2Place slip lead normally
Loop the slip lead over the dog's head as normal.
3Remove without reaching toward the head
To remove the lead, pull only the clip leash. The slip lead releases without reaching toward the dog's face or neck.
Back tie technique
#Step
1Attach carabiner to leash end
Clip a carabiner to the end of a leash.
2Secure to a fixed point
Clip to a fence or wrap the leash around an object and clip it to itself.
3Work safely at a distance
The dog is held stationary while you work, for example during pen restocking or vet checks.
Muzzle wrap technique

A rapid temporary restraint for situations where injury is anticipated and no other tools are immediately available, such as an injured dog in pain that needs to be moved.

#Step
1Place slip lead on neck
Place a slip lead onto the dog's neck.
2Wrap the snout
Quickly wrap the excess leash around the base of the dog's snout 2 to 3 times.
3Transition to basket muzzle
This is a temporary measure only. Switch to a properly fitted basket muzzle as soon as possible.

Enrichment reduces the idle time and boredom that drives baseline arousal. A dog that has had a lick mat for 30 minutes before your session is measurably calmer than one that has been pacing in an empty pen.

  • Prepare enrichment before you arrive. A dog that already has something to do when you approach has lower arousal from the start.
  • Puzzle feeders and kongs slow eating and engage the seeking drive.
  • Scent enrichment (snuffle mats, scent tubes) is calming and mentally tiring.
  • Nosework sessions are highly effective. Sniffing and searching are physiologically incompatible with peak arousal.
  • Physical exercise alone does not reduce arousal in highly reactive dogs. Mental stimulation and impulse control training are more effective.
See the Enrichment Volunteer Handbook for full setup guides. Sniff and Search Thursday is particularly well-suited to arousal dogs.
  • Do not toss treats into the pen while the dog is still jumping at the gate. Wait for 4 feet on the floor first.
  • Do not make eye contact, speak to, or reach toward a jumping or barking dog. This rewards the arousal.
  • Do not push through a session when the dog is over threshold.
  • Do not force a slip lead onto a highly aroused dog.
  • Do not match the dog's excitement with high-energy play. This escalates arousal.
  • Do not skip the row warm-up to save time.
  • Do not work an aroused dog during cleaning or feeding times if avoidable.

These dogs need patience, consistency, and the same approach from every person who interacts with them. Mixed signals slow progress significantly.
